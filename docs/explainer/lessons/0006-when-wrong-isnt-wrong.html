Lesson 6. When "Wrong" Isn't Wrong

The answer to the #1 skeptic question: "won't you flag innocent people?"

Last lesson: precision is "of the accounts we flagged, how many were guilty?" The scary kind of wrong is accusing an innocent. But here's the subtlety that turns the false-positive argument in your favor — and it's the most distinctive idea in the whole system.

The one idea

When the detector flags someone who isn't in this ring, there are two completely different cases — and only one is a real mistake.

Lumping them together makes the detector look worse than it is. The detector counts them separately, on purpose.

The two kinds of "not who we were looking for"

Analogy

Your security guard is chasing a specific shoplifter and stops the wrong person. Two stories:

Story A: the person they stopped turns out to be a different wanted criminal — a pickpocket with an open warrant. Off-target, sure, but they still caught a crook.

Story B: the person is a genuine, innocent shopper. That is the only stop that should cost you any trust.

Same surface fact ("not the one we were after"), opposite meaning.

The detector's three buckets

Every account in the flagged set lands in exactly one of three buckets:

  • This ring — a correct catch for the case at hand. A clean win.
  • Other-ring fraud — a member of a different fraud ring. Off the current case, but still a fraudster. Operationally, still a win. The detector calls this cross-ring bleed.
  • Legit — a genuine, innocent viewer. This is the real false positive — the only number that should ever scare you.

Two headline figures fall out: operational precision (flagged accounts that are fraud at all — this ring or another) and the legit false-positive rate (flagged accounts that are actually innocent). The whole system is tuned to keep that second number low.

Why this matters for your mission

The #1 question you'll get is "won't you flag innocent people?" The credibility-winning answer: "We split our off-target flags. Most of what looks like a 'wrong flag' is actually another ring's fraud — still fraud. The thing we guard hardest, and measure on its own, is the genuine-innocent rate." That single move separates a serious detector from a dragnet.

The detail that makes it honest

The synthetic world even plants benign super-fan clubs — innocent people who binge one creator together in a way that looks coordinated. They exist specifically to try to fool the detector. So the innocent-false-positive number is measured against the hardest innocents, not easy ones. When you quote that rate, you're quoting it under deliberate pressure.

The honest caveat — disclose it

Cross-ring bleed still drags down the simple per-case precision number from Lesson 5, even though it isn't a real error. So a single "precision" figure can make the detector look worse than it is — which is precisely why it reports the three-bucket breakdown instead of one tidy number. Refusing to hide behind one number is the trustworthy move.

Where this lives in the code: eval/evaluate.py buckets each flagged slot into flagged_this_ring / flagged_other_ring / flagged_legit, then derives operational_precision, legit_fp_rate, and cross_ring_frac. The all_fraud_ids roster is the "oracle" used only to sort flags into buckets — the detector never sees it.

🎙️ In one breath

Answer the skeptic's "what about false positives?" in ~20 seconds using the split. A good version:

"When we flag someone who isn't in the ring we're chasing, we don't just call it a mistake. Often they're in a different fraud ring — still a real catch. The only flag we treat as a true error is a genuine innocent viewer, and we measure that rate on its own — even against planted super-fan clubs designed to fool us."
