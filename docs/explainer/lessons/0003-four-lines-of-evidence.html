Lesson 3. Four Lines of Evidence

How the "rank" step actually decides who's most suspicious.

We've narrowed the field (Lesson 1) and learned that only rare shared clues count (Lesson 2). Now the second move: given a short list of candidates, how does the detector rank them so the most suspicious rise to the top?

The one idea

Judge every candidate on four independent lines of evidence,
then blend them into a single score.

No single clue convicts. The more lines that agree, the higher a candidate ranks.

The analogy: a hiring panel

Analogy

You don't hire someone off one interview. You combine, say, a coding test, their references, and a culture interview — and you weight them by how much you trust each one. Someone strong on all of them rises to the top of the stack; strong on one but weak on the others sits lower.

The detector's score is exactly this: a weighted vote of four "references" on each candidate.

The four references

  • Linkage (weight 45%) — "do they share rare infrastructure with the ring?" The rarity idea from Lesson 2. The detector's most-trusted reference.
  • Behavioral (weight 25%) — "do they act like the ring?" Same viewing patterns: skipping, view length, active hours, and so on. (Full deep-dive next lesson.)
  • Co-target (weight 15%) — "do they push the same obscure creator the ring is paid to inflate?" Overlap of the creators they view.
  • Action mix (weight 15%) — "do they use the product the way people do?" A real viewer searches, skips, saves and finishes some things; a crude bot goes straight to the target and "completes" everything. Plus account age: freshly minted accounts stand out.

How the blend works

Each reference produces a sub-score, all squeezed onto the same 0-to-1 scale so they're comparable (otherwise whichever one happens to use bigger raw numbers would dominate by accident). Then the detector adds them up by their weights:

The whole scoring formula, in words

score = 0.45 × linkage + 0.25 × behavioral + 0.15 × co-target + 0.15 × action mix

The weights are a statement of trust: linkage is the strongest reference; co-target and action mix the weakest. Compute that score for every candidate, sort highest-first, and that sorted list is the investigation's answer.

Why four instead of one?

Because any single signal can be evaded. A sophisticated ring rents fresh residential IPs and spoofs devices — that kills linkage. But it still has to view the target creator to get paid, so co-target survives. A clever bot might fake one behavior but not all ten at once. Four independent signals are far harder to beat simultaneously than any one alone. Being able to say "we don't rely on a single tell" is what makes the system credible to a skeptic.

The honest caveat — disclose this

In this version the weights (45/25/15/15) are hand-set by a human, not learned from data — a reasoned judgment call, not gospel. The learned rule (Lesson 7) is what replaces them when the adversary gets good. Saying so up front is a strength: it shows you know the difference between "we chose these" and "the data proved these."

Where this lives in the code: engine.py → score = w_linkage×linkage + w_behavioral×behavioral + w_cotarget×cotarget + w_actionmix×actionmix, with the weights 0.45 / 0.25 / 0.15 / 0.15 in config.py. Each Candidate keeps all four sub-scores, so you can always see why something ranked where it did.

Check yourself

Why combine four signals instead of trusting linkage alone?

A candidate scores high on co-target but near zero on everything else. Where do they land?

🎙️ In one breath

Name the four signals and the panel analogy in ~20 seconds. A good version:

"Each candidate gets judged like a job applicant by a panel: do they share rare infrastructure (linkage), do they behave like the ring (behavioral), and do they push the same target creator (co-target), and do they use the product like a person (action mix)? We weight those — linkage most — add them into one score, and rank. Four signals, because a ring can dodge one but rarely all four."
