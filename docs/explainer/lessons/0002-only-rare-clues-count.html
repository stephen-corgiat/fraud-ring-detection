Lesson 2. Only Rare Clues Count

What makes a shared clue actually mean something — the heart of the "narrow" step.

Last lesson: The detector narrows, then ranks. The narrow step pulls in accounts that "share something" with your seeds. But here's the catch a sharp viewer will immediately raise: everybody shares things. Millions of accounts use the same big phone networks. So what makes a shared clue actually mean something?

The one idea

A shared clue is only evidence when it's rare.

Common overlaps are coincidence and prove nothing. Rare overlaps demand an explanation — and "they're working together" is the explanation. The rarer the shared clue, the louder it speaks.

The analogy: the suspicious coincidence

Analogy

"We were both in France this year." So were millions of people — means nothing.

"We both stayed in the same eight-bed hostel in one tiny village, on the same night." That is not chance — you two are connected somehow.

Same fact ("we overlapped"), wildly different weight — and the only thing that changed is how rare the overlap is.

The detector's "clues" are technical fingerprints two accounts can share: an IP address, a network provider (ASN), a device fingerprint, a browser cookie. Sharing a giant mobile carrier is the "both in France" coincidence — tens of thousands of innocent people do. Sharing one obscure datacenter IP that only four accounts have ever touched is the tiny-hostel coincidence — that needs explaining.

Turning "rare" into a number

The detector gives every identifier a rarity score: small when lots of accounts share it, large when very few do. When two accounts share a high-rarity identifier, that's a strong link; sharing a low-rarity one barely moves the needle.

For the curious — one line of notation, then forget it

The score is literally log( total accounts ÷ accounts that share this clue ). If everyone shares it, that ratio is ~1 and the score is ~0 (no surprise). If 4-in-a-million share it, the ratio is huge and so is the score. This exact formula is a famous search-engine idea called IDF — it's how Google decides that "the" matters less than "the detector" in your query. You don't need the formula to explain it; "rarer = stronger" is the whole story.

The two-sided filter (this is the part skeptics love)

The detector won't even use an identifier unless its sharing count sits in a sensible middle band — here, between 2 and ~150 accounts:

  • Shared by only 1 account? Useless — there's nobody on the other end to link to.
  • Shared by 150+ accounts? Thrown out — that's a coffee shop's Wi-Fi or a big carrier, not a ring. This is the cut that stops innocent strangers from being framed for sharing public infrastructure.

Keep the rare-but-not-unique middle; ignore the rest. That single rule is most of why the detector's net is precise instead of a dragnet.

The honest caveat — say it before a skeptic does

"Couldn't a few innocent people share a rare IP by sheer bad luck?" Yes. Rarity makes a link strong, not certain. That's exactly why this is only one of four signals, and why the detector ranks suspects rather than convicting them. (The other three are next.)

Where this lives in the code: detector/ingest.py builds the identifier_stats table with rarity = ln(N / distinct_accounts). The two-sided filter is the distinct_accounts BETWEEN 2 AND 150 gate in engine.py (the numbers are expand_min_accounts / expand_max_accounts in config.py). This rarity score is the first scoring signal, called linkage.

🎙️ In one breath

Explain in ~15 seconds why a shared coffee-shop Wi-Fi isn't evidence but a shared rare device is. A good version:

"Sharing something common is just coincidence — like both being in France. Sharing something rare demands an explanation — like both being in the same tiny hostel the same night. So the detector only counts the rare overlaps, and ignores anything common enough to be public infrastructure."

📖 Primary source (optional): Stanford, Introduction to Information Retrieval — Inverse Document Frequency. The textbook origin of "rare = informative." Skim the first two paragraphs: their log(N/df) is the exact formula in your ingest.py.
