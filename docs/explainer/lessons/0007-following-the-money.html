Lesson 7. Following the Money

Why a clever adversary doesn't break the detector — the graceful-degradation story.

Every skeptic's last question: "Sure, but what about a smart fraudster who knows you're watching?" A sophisticated ring deliberately erases the trails we've relied on. This lesson is how The detector answers that — and it's the most reassuring idea in the whole system.

The one idea

A ring can hide how it operates, but not why it exists. When the infrastructure trail goes cold, follow the money instead.

The money is the one thing they can't fake away: to earn payouts, every member must keep viewing the same target creator.

How a sophisticated ring goes dark

Recall the linkage signal: it catches accounts that share rare infrastructure. So a careful ring stops sharing. Instead of a few datacenter IPs, each member rents residential proxies on the same big providers everyone's grandmother uses; each account sticks to just one or two IPs; every session mints a fresh device fingerprint. The shared footprint linkage needs simply isn't there anymore.

Analogy

A crime ring that stops sharing safe-houses, cars, and burner phones. Each member now lives a separate, ordinary-looking life. Your "who shares an address?" net comes up empty — every search returns a normal citizen.

The thing they can't hide

But the whole point of the ring is to pump one obscure target creator so the payouts flow. They can scatter their infrastructure infinitely — they cannot stop viewing the target, because that is the paycheck. So the detector switches nets:

The co-target net

Instead of "who shares rare infrastructure?", ask "who is all viewing this same obscure target, in the same campaign window?" The detector finds the target by spotting the creator the known seeds pour their payout-earning views into, then pulls in every account doing the same in that time window. The infrastructure trail is gone, but the money trail is wide open.

Analogy

You can't link the suspects by shared addresses anymore — so you follow the money. Who keeps depositing into the same account? That question doesn't care how clever they are about hiding where they live.

The detector fires this net adaptively — only when the seeds' shared infrastructure looks too thin to have worked (the sophisticated regime). On easy naive rings, where linkage already nails it, the co-target net stays holstered so it doesn't stir up needless noise.

The honest part: what "losing" actually looks like

As rings get more sophisticated, the ranking does get fuzzier — and here's how to describe that to a colleague without overclaiming, tying together Lesson 5 and Lesson 6:

  • The per-case ranking score (AP) slopes down — yes, it gets harder.
  • But the false-positive line holds — we don't start flagging innocent viewers.
  • The precision we "lose" is mostly cross-ring bleed — catching another ring's fraud, not smearing the innocent.

That's graceful degradation: under pressure the system gets less certain about which ring an account belongs to, not less certain about fraud vs. innocent. Being able to say that — precisely — is what separates an honest pitch from hype.

The caveat that proves you understand it

Following the money has a cost: the co-target net also scoops up genuine fans of that creator. Someone has to rank those fans back out — and the simple hand-weighted score can't, on its own. That's exactly why this wider net isn't always on, and why the next chapter of the system is a smarter, learned ranker. Retrieval and ranking have to advance together.

Where this lives in the code: generator/evasion.py → profile_for is the adversary dial (read the sophisticated profile: residential IPs/ASNs, 1–2 IPs per account). In engine.py, _seed_cohesion detects the thin linkage and _cotarget_candidates casts the money-trail net. The graceful-degradation curve is what eval/sweep.py plots.
