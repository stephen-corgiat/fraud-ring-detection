Lesson 4. Acting Alike

The behavioral signal — the one that sounds like scary math, defused.

Lesson 3 gave us four signals. The middle one — behavioral, "do they act like the ring?" — is the one that sounds the most mathematical (you may have heard the phrase "feature vectors in standardized space"). Let's take all the fear out of it. It's simpler than it sounds.

The one idea

Describe every account by a short stat line of numbers. A candidate whose stat line is close to the ring's typical stat line is acting like the ring.

The analogy: a sports player card

Analogy

Every basketball player is a row of stats: points, rebounds, assists, minutes, turnovers. Scouts spot "this rookie views like prime Steve Nash" by noticing the rookie's row of numbers sits close to Nash's row. No magic — just comparing two rows of numbers.

The detector does the identical thing, but the "player" is an account and the stats are viewing habits.

What's on an account's stat line

The detector measures about ten everyday behaviors per account — in plain words: how often they skip, how often a view lasts long enough to count for payouts, how many views per session, the average gap between actions and how regular that gap is, how many hours of the day they're active, how many different creators they touch, how fixated they are on one top creator, and how many IPs and networks they use. Bots tend to share a tell-tale stat line: barely skip, eerily regular gaps, a narrow set of creators.

The "average ring member"

Take the seed accounts' stat lines and average them column by column. That gives one composite typical ring member — the detector calls it the centroid, but "the average ring member" is all you ever need to say. Each candidate is then scored by how close their stat line sits to that average: close ⇒ high behavioral score, far ⇒ low.

The one fairness trick

Standardization, in plain words

Different stats live on wildly different scales — "views per session" might be 5 while "average gap between actions" might be 40,000 seconds. Compare those raw and the big-numbered stat drowns out everything else. So first, each stat is rephrased as "how unusual is this compared to the crowd?" — a little above average, or a lot below. Now every column speaks the same language and no single stat bullies the others. Think of it as grading every stat on a curve.

After that fair footing, "closeness" is just the straight-line distance between the two rows of numbers — like distance between two points on a map. Small distance becomes a score near 1; large distance, a score near 0.

The honest caveat

Real super-fans can act a bit like a ring — bingeing one creator on repeat looks robotic. So behavior alone is never proof. That's precisely why it's only 25% of the blend and always rides alongside linkage and co-target (Lesson 3).

Where this lives in the code: ingest.py builds account_features (the stat lines); the ten columns are behavioral_features in config.py. In engine.py, _standardizer does the "grade on a curve," the centroid is the average of the seed stat lines, and behav = 1 / (1 + distance) turns closeness into the 0-to-1 score.

🎙️ In one breath

Explain the behavioral signal with the player-card analogy in ~20 seconds. A good version:

"We describe each account by a stat line — skip rate, view length, how regular their timing is, and so on — like a sports player's box score. We average the known ring members into one typical stat line, then score each suspect by how close their stat line sits to it. We grade every stat on a curve first so no single big-numbered stat dominates."

📖 Primary source (optional): Google ML — Candidate Generation, the "similarity measures" part. It frames items as rows of numbers and compares them by distance — the same "close rows are similar" idea, in the recommender's language.
