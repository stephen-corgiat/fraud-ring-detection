Lesson 1. Narrow, then Rank

The one sentence your whole explanation hangs on — the spine of the detector.

The detector's job: someone hands you a few accounts you already know are cheating — a seed — and asks, "find the rest of the ring." There are millions of accounts in the system. How does the engine get from "3 known cheats" to "here are the other 40, ranked by how sure we are"?

The entire answer is two moves, in order.

The one idea

First narrow millions of accounts down to a short list.
Then rank that short list by strength of evidence.

That's it. Everything else is detail about how it narrows and how it ranks. The detector's code literally calls these two stages candidate selection (the narrow) and scoring (the rank).

The analogy: a detective with a few suspects

Analogy

You've arrested three members of a crime ring (your seeds). A whole city of innocent people surrounds them. You don't interrogate the entire city — that's impossible and cruel. Instead:

Move 1 — round up (narrow). Pull in everyone who shares something concrete with your three: same safe-house address, same burner phone, same getaway car. Fast, wide, cheap. You go from a million people to maybe fifty candidates.

Move 2 — build the case (rank). Now do the slow, careful work only on those fifty: weigh the evidence against each one and line them up, strongest case first.

The detector does exactly this. The "shared safe-house / phone / car" are shared technical fingerprints — IP addresses, devices, network providers. The "weigh the evidence and line them up" is the scoring step you'll meet in later lessons.

Why two separate moves? (the question a skeptic will ask)

Because the careful step is expensive and the world is huge. Weighing the full evidence against all million accounts would be hopelessly slow — and pointless, because almost none of them are connected to your seeds at all. So you spend the cheap, wide net first to throw away the 99.99% that are obviously irrelevant, and you spend the expensive judgment only on the survivors.

This narrow-then-rank shape isn't a quirk of this detector — it's how web search, recommendation feeds, and online-shop search all work under the hood. Narrow billions to hundreds cheaply, then rank the hundreds carefully. Being able to say "this is the same pattern as web search" makes your explanation instantly credible.

The one catch — say this before a skeptic does

The narrow step sets a ceiling. If a real ring member shares nothing with the seeds, the net never catches them — and no amount of clever ranking afterward can rank someone who was never pulled in. So "did we even retrieve them?" is a separate, earlier question from "did we rank them well?" the detector measures both, on purpose. (Later lesson: this is recall.)

Where this lives in the code: detector/engine.py → run_investigation(). The first SQL query builds a dictionary of candidates (the narrow). The for loop afterward gives each one a score and sorts (the rank). Two moves, one function — exactly the spine above.

🎙️ In one breath (this is the real win)

Try explaining it. Say the whole engine in under 20 seconds, in your own words, to an imaginary colleague. A good version sounds like:

"We start from a few accounts we already know are cheating. First we cast a wide, cheap net to pull in everyone connected to them — that's candidate selection. Then we carefully rank just those, strongest evidence first — that's scoring. Same narrow-then-rank trick web search uses."

Said it without peeking? That's Lesson 1 done. Stumbled? Say it twice more — fluency is the goal.
