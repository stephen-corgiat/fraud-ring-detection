Lesson 5. The Scorecard

How we prove the ranking is any good — the two numbers a skeptic will demand.

We can rank suspects (Lessons 1–4). But a sharp viewer won't take "trust us" for an answer. They'll ask: how do you know the ranking is any good? There are exactly two ways to be wrong, and the detector reports a number for each.

The one idea

Recall: of the real ring members, how many did we catch?
Precision: of the accounts we flagged, how many were actually guilty?

Recall is about misses (members we let slip). Precision is about mistakes (people we wrongly accused). Different failures, different numbers.

The analogy: the store security guard

Analogy

Recall = of all the actual shoplifters in the store today, what fraction did you stop? Low recall means thieves walked out.

Precision = of everyone you did stop, what fraction were actually shoplifting — versus innocent shoppers you embarrassed? Low precision means you're hassling honest customers.

Why you can't just maximize one

Here's the trap, and it's worth being able to say out loud:

  • Want 100% recall? Stop everyone leaving the store. You'll catch every shoplifter — and every innocent too. Precision collapses.
  • Want 100% precision? Stop only the one person you saw pocket something on camera. You'll never be wrong — but you'll miss everyone else. Recall collapses.

Good detection means keeping both high at once. That tension is the whole game — and quoting one number without the other is how dashboards lie.

How the detector grades itself (fairly)

You can only measure precision and recall if you already know the right answer. So the detector tests itself in a synthetic world where it secretly knows the truth: it hands the engine just 3 known members of a ring (the seeds), hides the rest, and says "find them." Then it checks the ranking against the hidden answer key. No human labeling required — which is the only reason any of this is measurable.

Because a real investigation can only act on so many accounts, the detector grades the top of the ranking: if 20 members were hidden, it looks at the top 20 and asks how many are real. That's recall@k ("did we find them?") and precision@k ("are our top picks right?") — precision and recall measured where it actually counts.

Tie-back to Lesson 1 — recall has two failure points

A member can be missed for two very different reasons: (1) the narrow step never retrieved them — the net itself missed them (Lesson 1's "ceiling"); or (2) it retrieved them but the rank step buried them too low. The detector reports the ceiling on its own (it calls it stage1_recall) so you can tell which stage to blame. "Did we even pull them in?" and "did we rank them well?" are separate questions.

Where this lives in the code: eval/evaluate.py — stage1_recall (the ceiling), average_precision (ranking quality), recall_at_k, and precision_at_k. The protocol hands over seeds_per_ring = 3 and hides the rest as the answer key.

🎙️ In one breath

Explain precision vs. recall with the security-guard analogy in ~20 seconds. A good version:

"Recall asks: of all the real shoplifters, how many did we stop? Precision asks: of everyone we stopped, how many were actually guilty? You can max either one alone — stop everyone, or stop nobody but your surest catch — so the real job is keeping both high. We prove it on a simulated world where we know the answer and hide it from the detector."
